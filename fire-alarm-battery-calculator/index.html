The method

How fire alarm battery capacity is worked out

If mains power fails, the standby batteries in a fire alarm panel or power supply unit have to do two jobs. First they keep the whole system running for the standby period, with every device drawing its normal quiescent current. Then they have to supply the full alarm load, with sounders and any other devices operating, for long enough to give a proper evacuation warning. The battery has to be big enough for both, with an allowance on top for ageing and temperature.

Capacity (Ah) = ( Iq × Tstandby + Ia × Talarm ) × 1.25
  • Iq is the quiescent current in amps: everything the system draws with no alarm active, including the panel itself, detectors, interfaces and any ancillary equipment.
  • Tstandby is the standby period in hours. 24 hours and 72 hours are the usual choices; your design specification and BS 5839-1 decide which applies.
  • Ia is the alarm current in amps with the system in alarm and all the devices that operate in an alarm drawing current.
  • Talarm is the alarm duration in hours. Thirty minutes (0.5 h) is the usual figure.
  • 1.25 is a 25% allowance for battery ageing and temperature, a commonly used design margin.

Worked example

A system with a quiescent current of 180 mA and an alarm current of 1.2 A, on a 24 hour standby with a 30 minute alarm:

( 0.18 A × 24 h + 1.2 A × 0.5 h ) × 1.25
= ( 4.32 + 0.60 ) × 1.25 = 6.15 Ah

The next standard size up is a 7 Ah battery. Change the standby period to 72 hours and the same system needs ( 0.18 × 72 + 0.6 ) × 1.25 = 16.95 Ah, so a 17 Ah battery. The standby period is usually what drives the size.

Standard battery sizes

The calculator rounds up to the next common sealed lead-acid (VRLA) size:

Panels with a 24 V supply normally use two 12 V batteries wired in series. The capacity in Ah is the same for each battery, so you still size to the figure above.

Before you rely on the number

What a calculator can't know

The sum is simple. Getting the inputs right is where batteries end up undersized.

Real current figures

Take the quiescent and alarm currents from the manufacturer's data for every device on the system, including loop devices, sounders, interfaces and ancillaries. An estimated quiescent current is the most common reason a battery turns out too small.

Charger limits

The panel or power supply has to recharge the batteries within the required time, and many have a maximum battery size they can charge. Check the manufacturer's data before specifying a larger battery.

Your specification

The standby and alarm periods come from the design, the system category and how quickly a fault can be put right. This tool is a guide for engineers, not a substitute for the design calculation.

FAQ

Common questions

How do I calculate the battery size for a fire alarm panel?

Add the energy needed for the standby period to the energy needed for the alarm period, then add a margin. In formula form: capacity in Ah = (quiescent current × standby hours + alarm current × alarm hours) × 1.25. Then choose the next standard battery size up. The calculator on this page does it for you and shows the working.

How many hours of standby does a fire alarm battery need?

It depends on the system design and how quickly a mains or battery fault can be dealt with. 24 hours is commonly used, with 72 hours where a fault could not be put right within a day. Always follow the specification for your system and BS 5839-1.

How long must the batteries support the alarm?

Thirty minutes at full alarm load is the usual figure, which is the default here. Some specifications call for more or less, so the calculator lets you choose 15, 30 or 60 minutes.

Can I use this instead of a design calculation?

No. It is a quick guide for engineers and installers. The design calculation, the manufacturer's current figures and the panel or power supply data take precedence, and the responsible person for the design signs off the final battery size.
